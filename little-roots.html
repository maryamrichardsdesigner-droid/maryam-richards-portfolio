01 — THE PROBLEM

It was never "not enough apps."

Every parent I spoke to already believed play mattered. They were stuck one step later — on what to actually do today, and whether any of it added up to something.

😮‍💨
"I don't know what to do today."
Decision fatigue at 7am. Pinterest saves nobody opens twice. The plan dies before breakfast.
STAY-AT-HOME PARENT
🗂
"I have activities. I have no structure."
Homeschoolers were drowning in one-off ideas with no term shape, no coverage view, nothing to show for it.
HOMESCHOOLER
🤝
"I want to show the parents what we did."
Au pairs and nannies had no shared language with the family for the day's play — just "she had a good day."
AU PAIR / NANNY
THE REFRAME
Three very different people, one identical gap: structure and planning — not content.

THE MOM TEST
If it needs a setup trip to the craft store, it's already dead.

Every activity had to run on what's already in the house — pasta, a muffin tin, pom poms, a banana. That's not a content constraint, it's the whole product thesis. The moment a parent has to buy something, the habit breaks.

NO SCORES NO CHECKLISTS NO GUILT
A MUFFIN TIN.
THAT'S IT.
THE FOUNDER TEST
One engine, sold four times — start where the pain is loudest and the sale is smallest.

Schools are the biggest cheque and the slowest close. Parents are the fastest yes. So the sequencing writes itself: earn the habit with parents first, let homeschoolers prove the curriculum depth, let au pairs & nannies prove the shared-language case — then walk into a school with all three already working.

Same engine, different surface
Parents get daily picks. Homeschoolers get term plans. Teachers get session blocks. One activity library and one planner underneath all of it.
Free tier is a wedge, not a demo
Deliberately narrow — enough free activities to build the swipe-and-play habit, not enough to replace the catalog.
THE DESIGNER TEST
Two frameworks that never spoke to each other.

The planner tags activities by 6 Pillars — Little Roots' own curriculum lens. Growth tracks children against 6 developmental Domains — the conventional framework families and schools recognise. Both are genuinely useful. But they were never mapped to each other, so a parent who planned a Move activity had no way to see it show up as Physical progress. The plan and the proof were two separate products in one app.

04 — THE WEDGE

Start with the loudest pain.
End with the biggest cheque.

Four audiences, deliberately sequenced. Each one earns the right to the next.

STAGE 01 · MVP TESTED
Moms & parents
Fastest yes, hardest habit. Daily pillar-balanced picks, Learning Stories, Growth View.
STAGE 02 · MVP TESTED
Homeschoolers
Proves curriculum depth. Weekly plan grid, theme system, term portfolio & work samples.
STAGE 03 · MVP TESTED
Au pairs & nannies
Proves the shared-language case — a caregiver and a parent finally describing the day the same way.
STAGE 04 · NEXT PHASE
Schools
Biggest cheque, slowest close. Session blocks, class-wide stories, pillar coverage reporting.
WHY THIS ORDER
A school won't buy a planning tool that no parent has ever used. Every earlier stage is evidence for the next sales conversation.
05 — WHAT THE MVP TAUGHT ME

Tested with the people who'd actually open it.

Stay-at-home parents, homeschoolers and au pairs — not a focus group of people who don't live this.

WHAT LANDED
"Things I already have"
The single most repeated reaction. It removed the excuse before it formed.
Mess level, up front
Being honest about cleanup built more trust than any activity photo did.
No scores
Parents relaxed the moment they understood nothing was being graded — theirs or the child's.
WHAT DIDN'T
"Montessori play, one swipe away"
The shipped v1 line. Fast to market, but it boxed the product into one pedagogy's audience and read like every other listing.
Plan and proof were separate
Pillars in the planner, domains in Growth, no mapping between them — the app quietly asked users to hold two taxonomies at once.
Browsing without a cap
Before the "one primary + max three secondary" rule, every activity matched everything. Filters became useless.
THE REPOSITION THAT CAME OUT OF IT
From "a Montessori activity app"
to an AI platform for whole-child development.
Same 134 activities. Informed by Montessori, Reggio Emilia, Waldorf and Forest School — tied to none of them.
06 — PRODUCT THINKING

Why, not just what.

This one is early-stage and I'd rather say so than dress a hypothesis up as a result.

WHY IT EXISTS
Parents don't need another content library — they need a decision made for the exact child in front of them, right now. Swipe deck, age bands and mess filters all exist to answer "what do I do in the next ten seconds," not to be browsed like a catalog.
SUCCESS METRIC
Not downloads. Time-from-open to activity-started — everything about age-matching and mess filtering is designed against that one number. It isn't instrumented yet, and that's the next build.
TRADE-OFF
Hand-crafted animation for every activity would look better. Chose 8 reusable templates × 10 rotating characters for ~90% of the catalog — consistency and shipping speed over per-activity polish, because a solo founder can't art-direct 134 bespoke scenes.
PRIORITISATION
Parents → homeschoolers → au pairs & nannies → schools. Loudest pain and smallest sale first; the biggest, slowest buyer last, armed with evidence from all three earlier groups.
EXPERIMENT
The narrow free tier is a live pricing experiment, not a researched number. Same with the "one primary + max three secondary" pillar cap — a rule invented to keep filters meaningful, still to be validated at scale.
WHAT DIDN'T WORK
Shipping under Montessori-only branding. And letting two taxonomies — pillars and domains — coexist without a mapping, which silently broke the link between planning and proof.
WHAT I'D DO NEXT
Instrument the one metric that matters, then earn the school conversation: pillar coverage reporting is the artifact a head of school will ask for, and it's the one thing the parent-first strategy hasn't had to build yet.
